\section{Data and backups}
\label{sec:data}

The course defines data by what is lost if it disappears
\parencite{llusa2026volumes}. Critical data must be backed up. Volatile
data can be recomputed, even if slowly. Configuration and code are not
data: they belong in images, environment variables, configs or secrets,
and they live in git. \Cref{tab:data} classifies everything the
infrastructure stores with that rule.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{What the infrastructure stores, where, and how it is protected.}
  \label{tab:data}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llll}
    \toprule
    Data                   & Where                                 & Class         & Protection \\
    \midrule
    App database           & PostgreSQL, NFS share                 & critical      & nightly dump \\
    Moodle database        & MariaDB, \texttt{swarm-manager-3} disk & critical      & nightly dump \\
    Moodle files           & NFS share                             & critical      & nightly archive \\
    Registry images        & NFS share                             & recomputable  & rebuilt from git \\
    TLS certificates       & \texttt{swarm-manager-1} disk         & recomputable  & reissued by certbot \\
    Caches                 & Redis, Moodle cache folders           & volatile      & rebuilt on demand \\
    Configuration, code    & git, secret files                     & configuration & versioned in git \\
    Container logs         & each node                             & operational   & rotated in atenea only \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\Cref{tab:backups} shows the two backup jobs. Each one checks its files
before keeping them, because a backup that cannot be read is not a
backup, and the course stresses that backups only count once a restore
has been tested \parencite{llusa2026hadr}. Both jobs write to the NFS
share, in \texttt{/srv/nfs/<stack>/backups}, and each stack has a restore
script next to its backup script. The Moodle restore was timed at
\qty{21}{\second} (\cref{sec:stack-atenea}). The app's restore was not
timed.

\begin{table}[htbp]
  \centering
  \caption{Backups of the critical data. Times are UTC.}
  \label{tab:backups}
  \small
  \begin{tabular}{llllc}
    \toprule
    Stack  & Content                                  & When          & Checked with                          & Kept \\
    \midrule
    app    & \texttt{pg\_dump}, custom format         & 03:00 daily   & \texttt{pg\_restore --list}           & 7 days \\
    atenea & \texttt{mariadb-dump} and file archive   & 03:30 daily   & \texttt{gzip -t}, \texttt{tar -t}     & 7 days \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Container logs stay on the node that runs the container. The atenea
stack keeps at most three files of \qty{10}{\mega\byte} per container.
The other stacks use Docker's default, which never rotates, so their logs
can fill a disk over time.

The weak point is where the backups live. Every backup sits on the NFS
share, which is the disk of \texttt{swarm-manager-1}, and the app's
database lives there too. Losing that disk would take the data and its
backups together. A nightly copy of the backup folders to storage outside
Azure was planned but not built, and it is the first next step
(\cref{sec:conclusions}).
